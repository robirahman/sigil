# Know Your Spells: A Tier List, Synergies, and Matchups for Sigil

*Draft for the publisher's blog. Ratings are from our own play experience; the statistics come from 2,411 completed games on Sigil Online.*

Every game of Sigil deals nine spells onto the board: three rituals (five nodes), three sorceries (three nodes), and three charms (one node). Both players can see them from the first move, and both can fill and cast any of them. The spells themselves are the shared terrain of the game, so knowing which ones are worth fighting for, which ones work together, and which ones beat which is most of what separates a strong opener from a weak one.

This article covers the 39 spells in the core set and the eight expansion packs (Springtime, Celestial, Inferno, Tempest, Flood, Autumn, Gloom, and Covenant). It has four parts: a tier list, a guide to which spells prefer open or crowded boards, a table of synergies and anti-synergies, and a table of head-to-head matchups. A final section looks at what the recorded games say, and where they disagree with us.

A reminder on two rules that come up repeatedly below. Casting a spell locks it for you until your lock moves, and you get one cast per turn unless you hold Seal of Summer. Red moves first, and to compensate, red needs a three-stone lead to win while blue needs only two.

A few notes on the placements that tend to surprise people.

**Gather and Harvest are at the top because they work with everything.** On their own they do nothing except refill themselves. But cast any other non-static spell first and then Gather or Harvest, and the second cast refills the first spell, unlocks it, and nets you a stone in the process. Paired with a soft-move spell they become an engine: the two spells refill each other, you cast one every turn, and once both are filled you gain two or three stones over four to six turns. The fastest win we know of uses exactly this (see the end of the article).

**Fireblast rewards patience.** It destroys every enemy stone touching yours, then costs you one stone. On a board where the two sides are in contact in many places it can swing the game by four or five stones in one cast. Its weakness is that building that contact takes time, which is why Carnage beats it head to head (see Matchups).

**Scatter and Blossom are top tier for how they combine.** Both drop stones into several spells at once, which both develops the board and sets up Erupt (two moves into every spell you already have a stone in). Their weakness is a specific counter: Hail Storm destroys one enemy stone in every three- and five-node spell, and Decay destroys every enemy stone touching two or more empty nodes. Against either, a freshly cast Blossom can be wiped in one turn.

**Charge and Slash are the best charms.** A one-node spell that reliably places or pushes a stone is worth more than its size suggests, because a charm can be recast every turn you can refill it. Slash is the strongest charm in the core set; Charge (a move into any three- or five-node spell) is the strongest overall.

**Grow is near the bottom, despite being a fine partner.** Two soft moves for a three-node investment does not net stones. Grow is good in one situation only: next to Harvest, where the pair refill each other. Torrent, one soft move then one hard move, is the weakest spell in the game.

**The static seals are mostly weak.** Seal of Wind is the exception and sits in the strong tier: a blink for your first move every turn is real mobility. Seal of Lightning (dash for one sacrifice instead of two) is solid. Seal of Summer looks powerful on paper, but two casts a turn is only useful if you have the stones to fill two spells. Seal of Destruction is a special case: it destroys everything touching you at the end of your turn, but if it is still filled at the start of your next turn you lose, and only your opponent moves in between. It is a finisher, not a spell you develop toward, and it is much stronger for blue, who can win from a two-stone lead by bordering two enemy stones and filling the seal.

## Open board or crowded board?

Some spells want empty space and some want contact. Where a spell falls on this axis tells you when in the game to fill it.

The clearest cases are the two destruction sorceries. Fireblast wants as many points of contact as possible, so it is at its best late, when the board is full. Decay destroys enemy stones touching two or more empty nodes, so it wants the opposite: an early board with thin, spread-out formations.

Two entries deserve a comment. Bewitch converts two touching enemy stones, so you might expect it to want a crowded board. It does not need one: every turn starts with a regular move that places a stone next to one of your own, so you almost always end your turn with a touching pair. The only way to deny Bewitch its targets is to dash and sacrifice every turn, which costs more than being Bewitched. Bewitch's real value is on an open board, where converting two stones swings territory.

Hurricane destroys the smallest contiguous enemy group. Early in the game your opponent may have a single group, and a Hurricane then removes all of it. On a crowded board the groups are already split and Hurricane only picks off stragglers. We list it as no preference because its real dependency is not crowding but group structure, which the opponent controls (see Matchups).

## Synergies

Two spells have synergy when holding both lets you do something neither does alone. Three general principles explain most of the table.

**Refill loops.** Casting a spell locks it. Gather and Harvest place stones into your locked spell, so cast a spell and follow with Gather or Harvest and you have both refilled the first spell and gained a stone. Soft-move spells are the best partners, because they can refill Gather or Harvest in turn. This is the strongest synergy in the game. Gather and Harvest have no synergy with each other, because refilling each other nets nothing.

**Destroying and converting are redundant.** Every enemy stone Hail Storm or Meteor removes is one Bewitch or Corrupt can no longer convert. Both effects are good, but they eat each other's targets. Two destruction spells cast on consecutive turns thin each other out the same way.

**You only need to reach a mana once.** Meteor, Blossom, Seal of Wind, Comet, and Flourish all help you cover distance. Holding two of them is wasteful: once you have reached a distant mana, the second reach spell has nothing to do, unless the opponent retakes the area.

There is also a small, universal anti-synergy: with one cast per turn, any two spells compete for the same casting opportunity. We ignore it in the table, except where it is the main effect.

Some of the entries worth explaining:

- Blossom or Scatter, then Erupt. The first seeds a stone into several spells; Erupt then makes two moves into every spell you have a stone in.
- Seal of Lightning and Surge. Lightning makes dashing cost one sacrifice; Surge gives a free move whenever you dashed. Together, a cheap dash every turn pays for itself.
- Gust and Decay. Gust picks up every enemy stone touching you and drops them wherever you like. Drop them in isolation and Decay destroys them all next turn.
- Comet and Flourish. Comet's blink can grab a mana; Flourish's four soft moves then convert two or more mana into a stone advantage. Comet pairs well with many spells for the same reason: it converts a blink into mana, and most spells are better with more mana.
- Bewitch and Fireblast. Bewitch converts the two stones at the front of an enemy formation; the rest of it is now touching you.

The stories behind the strongest entries:

**Hurricane loses to any charm that makes a move.** Hurricane destroys the smallest contiguous enemy group, which is devastating against a single group. The defence is cheap: place stones across a charm's node, then cast the charm. The vacated node splits your stones into two groups, and Hurricane now removes only the smaller one. Any charm that lets you place a stone (Sprout, Slash, Surge, Splash, Charge, Azimuth, Lurk) does this. Hurricane is far stronger for red, whose tempo lets it threaten the one-shot before blue can arrange a split; blue usually has to dash and sacrifice to stay safe.

**Hail Storm and Decay punish spread.** Blossom places one stone in each other three- and five-node spell, which is exactly one target per spell for Hail Storm. Those same lonely stones each touch two or more empty nodes, which is Decay's condition. Scatter has the same problem against Decay.

**Carnage beats Fireblast.** Fireblast needs many bordering stones to be worth its cost, and building that contact takes turns. Carnage's four hard moves can seize the contested area, or the Fireblast spell itself, before the buildup pays off. Comet beats Carnage and Bewitch the same way: its blink lets you avoid contact, and both of those spells need contact to do anything.

**Seal of Lightning beats Seal of Stone.** Seal of Stone forces your opponent's regular move to be soft, but dashes and spell casts still push and destroy. Lightning makes the dash cheap, so the restriction hardly bites. For the same reason Seal of Stone does not really counter Carnage, Fury, or Slash. Seal of Autumn, which forbids sacrificing stones in spells to dash, is the real answer to Lightning.

**Storm Front and Meteor do not counter seals.** In theory knocking one stone out of a static seal switches it off. In practice the stone is easy to replace and the tempo is rarely worth it, so we rate those matchups even. Fissure, in the Tectonic pack (not covered here), is the real seal counter because its void disables the spell permanently.

## What the recorded games say

We also looked at 2,411 completed games on Sigil Online. A word of caution first: only 21 of them are between two human players. Nearly all involve one of the site's AI opponents on at least one side, so the statistics reflect AI habits as much as human ones.

The obvious statistic is "how often does the player who cast spell X win?" It is also the most misleading one, because you only cast a spell when it is useful. A player who invests four moves into Hurricane and is then neutralised by a split group never casts it, and probably loses, and that game never shows up in Hurricane's numbers. So we looked at three views:

- (a) win rate of the side that cast the spell;
- (b) win rate of the side that started closer to the spell, once both players had a stone down;
- (c) win rate of the side with more stones in the spell after blue's fifth turn.

Views (b) and (c) measure commitment rather than payoff.

| Spell | (a) when cast | (b) started closer | (c) more stones at turn 10 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hurricane | 94% | 59% | 51% |
| Surge | 96% | 54% | 39% |
| Fireblast | 82% | 51% | 70% |
| Seal of Wind | n/a | 55% | 72% |
| Scatter | 74% | 69% | 77% |
| Hail Storm | 64% | 51% | 59% |
| Carnage | 65% | 53% | 51% |
| Bewitch | 64% | 51% | 45% |
| Starfall | 61% | 50% | 44% |
| Flourish | 54% | 49% | 41% |
| Seal of Lightning | n/a | 51% | 44% |
| Comet | 44% | 52% | 63% |

Four things stand out.

**Selection bias is real, and largest for Hurricane and Surge.** Hurricane wins 94% of games when cast and 51% for the side that invests in it. Surge wins 96% when cast, because casting it means you already dashed profitably, but the side that commits to it early wins only 39%.

**Fireblast and Seal of Wind reward early commitment.** They are the two spells where simply having more stones in them at turn 10 predicts winning, at 70% and 72% over hundreds of games each. If you are going to claim a spell early, claim one of these.

**Leading in a ritual early predicts losing.** The side with more stones in Flourish, Starfall, Bewitch, or Seal of Lightning at turn 10 wins only 41% to 45% of the time, across 650 or more games each. Five nodes is a lot of tempo to spend before the payoff.

**The spell layout is fair.** In standard games the nine spells are placed at random around the fixed starting positions. Over 900 games per spell, the side that starts closer to any given spell wins between 47% and 52%. No spell placement favours either player.

Where the data and our ratings disagree, we side with experience. The data rates Decay, Tsunami, and Gust higher than we do, on samples of 18, 43, and 4 casters respectively. It rates the charms as roughly interchangeable, because each side tends to hold whichever charm sits in its territory, so cast counts reflect geography rather than choice.

## The fastest win

To close, here is how the refill loop plays out at full speed. Suppose the layout puts Harvest, Sprout, and Grow in a line from blue's starting mana, and red does not interfere.

1. Turns 1 to 3: spread through Harvest.
2. Turn 4: place in Sprout.
3. Turn 5: place in Grow, and cast Sprout to place Grow's second node.
4. Turn 6: fill Grow's third node and cast it, completing Harvest.
5. Turn 7: place in Grow and cast Harvest, netting one stone.
6. Turn 8: cast Grow.
7. Turn 9: cast Harvest again. Blue is two stones ahead and wins.

Nine turns, no opponent blunder required beyond leaving the engine alone. As far as we know this is the fastest win in the game that does not rely on the other player sacrificing stones or walking into a Hurricane or Seal of Destruction one-shot. It is also the clearest argument for why Gather and Harvest sit at the top of the tier list, and why, if you see this layout on your opponent's side of the board, you should go and break it up.
